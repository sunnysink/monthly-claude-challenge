The Discovery Call Companion

Everything about the call itself: what to ask, what you are listening for, and where the yes actually happens. Fill it in for one real person, then keep it open while you are on the call.
You can be brilliant at booking discovery calls and still hear no every time. The call is usually the part that was already working.

I once did sixty one hour discovery calls in a row and got sixty nos. I kept the same pitch, the same words and the same offer for all sixty, because I had been taught to keep trying. The calls were fine. The offer they pointed at was the problem, and so was everything I did after we hung up.

So this covers all three parts, in the order they decide the outcome. Start on The Offer. If that tab is not clear, no script can save it.

The essentials
Six things. When one of these is missing, the call spends its energy fixing something an hour at your desk would have handled properly.
I can say what changes for them in one sentence, out loud, without backing up Not what it includes. What is different for them at the end of it. Say it to the wall right now. If you need a run-up, a bit of history, or the word "basically," the sentence is not there yet, and on a call you get one try at it while your heart is going.
Every deliverable has a name, and there is one for every week A twelve week program needs twelve things they can point at. And each one needs a real name, ideally a line describing it. "Weekly support materials" is a shrug. "The Sovereign Leader Transformation Guide" is a thing that exists, and named things are what your mockups are pictures of.
There is a page they can look at after we hang up They are going to want to reread it, sit with it, or show someone. When the only place your offer exists is your voice on a call, it disappears the moment the call ends. A Canva page with a Stripe checkout link on it is plenty. A Google Doc will do in a pinch. This does not need to be a funnel.
That page shows what they actually receive, as images Mockups of the guide, the portal, the deck, the workbook, whatever it is. A bulleted list of deliverables reads as a promise. A picture of the thing reads as something that already exists, which is the difference between hoping and buying.
I can say the price out loud at a normal volume Practise it in the car. The mumbled price, the price wrapped in an apology, and the phrase "let's talk about investment" all say the same thing about what you believe it is worth.
There is a reason to decide now, and it has a clock on it The clock is the whole thing. A quick pay discount if they pay within 48 hours. A bonus call that goes to whoever books by Friday. A start date that fills. A bonus with no deadline on it is available forever, which means it gives nobody a reason to answer you today. Make it true, put an hour on it, and say it out loud on the call.
The rest
These make the call easier. None of them will sink it on their own.
I can name the person it is not for An offer for everyone is an offer nobody recognises as theirs. Naming who it is not for is what makes the person on the call certain that it is them.
I know what happens in their first week "We would start with a session" is vague enough to feel risky. "Monday you fill in the intake, Wednesday we meet, and by Friday you have your first one written" is a plan, and a plan is easy to say yes to.
I know the objection I am going to hear, because I have heard it before Money, timing, or a partner who needs convincing. It is nearly always one of those three. Decide your honest answer while you are calm, rather than inventing one while they wait.
I know what I say when they are not a fit You will get people this is wrong for, and the call goes better when you already have the sentence. Having somewhere else to send them turns it into a kindness.
I know how they pay, today, without going to look it up The link, the plan option, the deposit. Any gap here turns a yes into "I'll send that over later," and later is where yeses go to die.
⭐ One thing to decide on purpose: whether your price goes on the booking form. Put it there and everybody who books has already decided they can spend that much, so you get fewer calls and they are further along. Leave it off and you get more calls, some of which were never going to be able to say yes. Neither one is the right answer. When your calendar is empty and your time is free, more calls is the better trade. When your calendar is full, it stops being.

Open
The lines in the cream boxes are a script you can read word for word until it becomes yours. The questions underneath are the part that matters, and the order they are in is doing most of the work.
01

Building rapport

3 minutes
They have probably been nervous about this since they booked it. Your only job here is to make it a conversation rather than an audition. Tell them how long you have and what you are going to do with it, so they stop guessing.
Say this

"Hi, I am so glad you booked this. Before we get into anything, how are you doing today?"

"So we have got thirty minutes. What I would like to do is spend most of it understanding where you are, and then I will tell you honestly whether I am the right person for it. Does that sound alright?"

"How are you doing today?"
Listening for: whether they are rushed, distracted, or already braced for a pitch. If it is any of those, name it and slow down. Nobody buys while they are holding their breath.
"I will tell you honestly whether I am the right person for it."
Why it matters: it tells them there is a point at which you might say no. That one sentence takes the pressure out of the whole call, and it makes your yes mean something at the end.
Where it goes wrong

Filling the silence with your own story. They booked to be understood, and the first four minutes decide which of you the call is about.

02

Where they are, and where they want to be

12 minutes
Most of the call lives here, and the order is doing the work. Open it with why they came, then the hurt, then what they actually want, then what stands in the way. Everything after this gets measured against their picture instead of your offer.
Open with this

"So tell me, what inspired you to book this call with me today?"

The opening
"What inspired you to book this call with me today?"
Something happened recently, and this asks for it directly. People book calls on the day the problem finally costs them something, and whatever that was is the thing they actually want solved. Ask it, then let them run for as long as they want to.
Step 1 · The pain
"What has been the hardest part of that for you?"
Take notes while they answer, and mirror it back in their exact words as you go. Not your summary of it, their sentence. It is the fastest way to make somebody feel seen, and it makes the rest of the call a different conversation.

Listen for the specific version. The tidy version is the one they have already told six people, and the real thing usually arrives after a silence you had to sit through.
Step 2 · The desired outcome
"If we were to work together, what would you hope the outcome would be after [10, 12, however many] weeks?"
Put them at the end of your program, not at a magic tomorrow. They picture the real timeline, which is the one you would actually be working in.

This is also the question that tells you whether to make an offer at all. You are never promising an outcome. What you are checking is whether you can say, honestly and with a straight back, that your program is built to get somebody there. If the answer is no, this is where you find out, and it is a gift.

Write their answer down word for word. You will say it back in phase three and use it again in phase four, and their sentence lands harder than anything on your page.
Step 3 · Underneath it
"And what would that change for you?"
The thing under the thing. Fully booked is the outcome. Sleeping through the night is what they are buying. Ask it a second time if the first answer is still a business answer.
Step 4 · The gap
"What do you think is in the way of that right now?"
Listening for: their own diagnosis. It is often wrong, and that is useful. Where their diagnosis and yours differ is exactly what your offer has to answer in phase four.
Step 5 · What they have tried
"What have you already tried?"
Listening for: the thing you are about to describe. If they have tried something close to your offer, ask what part of it did not work. That answer is the whole call.
Step 6 · The cost of staying
"If nothing changes, where does that leave you in a year?"
The question most people skip, and the one that moves someone. Ask it gently and then be quiet. They have usually never said the answer out loud, and hearing themselves say it does more than any argument you could make.
Step 7 · The open window
"Is there anything you would love to share with me that you have not yet, about your situation?"
Leave this wide open and wait. It is the only question with no direction in it, and it is where the real thing tends to arrive: the health thing, the money thing, the person at home. Whatever comes out here belongs in your offer two minutes later. Say it back to them by name and the offer stops sounding like a package.
Where it goes wrong

Hearing a problem you can solve and jumping to the solution at minute six. The moment you start describing your offer, they stop telling you things, and everything they had not said yet is gone. Step 7 exists because that door closes and never reopens.

03

Say it back

2 minutes
Repeat their situation to them and have them confirm it. The highest value two minutes of the call, and the part most people skip. Use their words, including whatever arrived in step 7.
Say this

"Let me make sure I have got this. Right now [what is hardest]. What you actually want is [their outcome, in their words], and the reason that matters is [what they said it would change]. And the thing in the way is [the gap]. Have I got that right?"

"Is there anything I have missed?"

"Have I got that right?"
Listening for: "yes, exactly" or a correction. Both are good. A correction here saves you from describing the wrong thing thirty seconds later.
"Is there anything I have missed?"
Listening for: anything still being held back. It often arrives right here, once they can tell you were genuinely listening.
Where it goes wrong

Saying it back in your marketing language instead of theirs. People recognise their own sentences and not your positioning, and swapping them in makes the whole thing feel handled.

04

Offer it, once

6 minutes
Now you describe the thing. Their outcome, then the plan, then the first week, then the price, then the thing with the clock on it, then stop talking. The stopping is the hard part and it is the part that works.
Say this

"Based on everything you have told me, here is what I would do with you. By the end of it, [their outcome, in their words]. The way we get there is [the plan, in three steps]. In your first week, [the concrete first week]."

"It is [price]. And if you decide within the next 48 hours, [the discount, or the bonus]. Either way I am going to send you the page with all of it on it."

[Then stop. Count to ten.]

"What is coming up for you as I say that?"

Their outcome, in their language, before anything else
Two minutes at most. Their words, then the shape of the plan, then the first week concretely. Deliverables come last, and only the named ones. Work in whatever they gave you in step 7.
"It is [price]. And if you decide within the next 48 hours, [the incentive]."
Then say nothing. Count to ten. The silence after a price feels like it lasts a year and it is where they decide. Filling it with a discount you had not planned, an extra bonus, or a nervous joke is how the last three seconds undo the whole call.
"What is coming up for you as I say that?"
Listening for: the real objection, which is almost never the first one. "I need to think about it" usually means money, timing, or a person they need to talk to. Ask which one and they will usually tell you.
Where it goes wrong

Offering three options. Two choices is a decision and three is a research project, and a research project ends with "let me get back to you."

05

Ask them to start, while they are still on the call

4 minutes
Take the payment on the call. That is the goal of the whole thirty minutes. Nobody is ever more certain than they are right now, with your voice in their ear and their own words still hanging in the air. Have the link open in a tab before the call starts, ask them directly, and stay on while they pay.
Say this

"So how do you feel about starting?"

[If yes] "Wonderful. I am going to drop the link in the chat right now and we can do it while we are still on. It takes about a minute. Then I will tell you exactly what happens next."

[If they need to think] "Completely fair. Can I ask what you need to think about? Sometimes it is the money, sometimes it is the timing, and sometimes it is something I have not explained well."

[Then] "Shall I send you the page and check back with you on [a specific day]? Remember the [discount or bonus] is good until [the deadline]."

[If no] "That is genuinely fine. Would it help if I pointed you somewhere else?"

⭐ The one that matters
"Let's do it now while we are on the call."
Payment on the call is the number one goal, every time. Send the link, stay on the line, and use the ninety seconds while they type to tell them what happens Monday. A yes that leaves the room becomes an email, then a maybe, then nothing, and none of that is about how much they wanted it.
"How do you feel about starting?"
Ask it and stop. This is the question the whole call was for, and most people talk straight past it into logistics. It is a soft question with a real answer inside it.
"Can I ask what you need to think about?"
Listening for: which of the three it is. Money, timing, or someone they need to talk to. Each one has a different honest answer, and guessing which gets you the wrong one.
"Shall I check back with you on [a specific day]?"
A named day makes the follow-up something they agreed to rather than something you are doing at them. Put it in your calendar while they are still on the line, and put it in the log. Say the deadline on your incentive out loud again here.
Where it goes wrong

Ending warm and vague. They felt great, you felt great, and neither of you agreed on anything. Warm and specific is the same call with a date in it.

After the callsame day, then the day you named
Same day, within a couple of hours: the page, and three lines. Their outcome in their words, one line on what you would do about it, the deadline on your incentive, and the day you agreed to check back. Nothing else, and no new arguments. The prompts tab writes this from your call notes.

On the day you named: one message that refers to the specific thing they told you. Not "just checking in." Closer to "you said you wanted the readings booked before September, and I have been thinking about that."

Then stop. Two touches and a genuine door left open does better than six touches and a person who now avoids your name in their inbox.
⭐ Send the page even when it is a no. Nos change when circumstances change, and the only people who come back are the ones who still have the link.
